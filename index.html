Roof Terms Explained
Class A, B, C Roof Rating
A fire-resistance rating assigned to roofing materials. Class A is the most fire-resistant (clay tile, metal, asphalt shingles). Class B is moderate. Class C is the least resistant. Wood shake roofs with no fire treatment are typically Class C and are one of the biggest fire risks in LA.
Attic Vents
Small openings on your roof or under your eaves that allow air to circulate in your attic. During a wildfire, embers float through the air and can enter through unscreened vents, landing inside your attic and starting a fire from within. Ember-resistant vents have a very fine metal mesh (1/16 inch) that blocks embers while still allowing airflow.
Eaves
The part of the roof that overhangs the walls of your house — the underside of the roof edge. Open eaves have exposed wooden rafters visible from below, which can catch embers. Enclosed (boxed) eaves have a flat soffit panel covering the rafters, blocking ember entry.
Wood Shake
Hand-split wooden shingles, usually made from cedar. They give roofs a rustic look but are highly flammable and were banned in many LA hillside areas after the 1991 Oakland Hills fire. If your home was built before the 1990s, check carefully — you may still have a wood shake roof.
How to Find Out Your Roof Type
1. Look from the ground
Stand in front of your home and look at the roof. Clay or concrete tiles are curved or flat rectangular pieces, often terracotta colored. Metal roofs have visible seams or panels. Asphalt shingles look like overlapping flat rectangles. Wood shake looks rough, uneven, and weathered gray-brown.
2. Check your home inspection report
If you purchased or rented your home recently, the inspection report will list the roof type, age, and condition. Look for a PDF in your email or paperwork from your real estate transaction.
3. Ask your landlord or property manager
If you rent, your landlord is required to know basic facts about the property. Send a quick email or text asking: "What type of roofing material does the building have, and approximately how old is the roof?"
4. Look up your property on the LA County Assessor
Go to assessor.lacounty.gov, enter your address, and look at the property details. It often lists construction materials including roof type. This is free and public information.
5. Check for attic vents
Walk around your home and look for small rectangular or triangular grilles under the eave overhangs or on the roof. If you can see through them easily (mesh looks large), those are standard vents. If they have very fine metal mesh, they may already be ember-resistant. If unsure, select "Don't know" — it's better to be cautious.
Structure Terms Explained
Stucco
A cement-based plaster applied to the outside of walls, giving them a textured, sand-like finish. Very common in LA. Stucco is non-combustible and one of the best wall materials for fire resistance. Most Spanish-style and mid-century homes in LA have stucco exteriors.
Masonry
Construction using brick, stone, concrete blocks, or similar materials. Masonry walls are non-combustible and highly fire-resistant. You can usually identify them by the visible brick or block pattern, or by the solid, hard feel of the wall surface.
Fiber Cement (Hardie Board)
A manufactured material made from cement, sand, and cellulose fibers, used as siding panels. It looks like wood but is much more fire-resistant. James Hardie is the most common brand. It's often used in newer construction or renovations as a safer alternative to wood siding.
Wood Siding / Lap Board
Horizontal wooden planks attached to the exterior of the house. Highly combustible — direct flame or radiant heat from a nearby fire can ignite wood siding quickly. Common in older craftsman bungalows and some mid-century homes.
Vinyl Siding
Plastic panels used as exterior cladding. While vinyl doesn't easily ignite from a spark, it melts and deforms under intense radiant heat, which can expose the underlying structure. More common in newer apartment buildings than single-family homes in LA.
WUI Building Codes (2008)
WUI stands for Wildland-Urban Interface — areas where buildings and wildland vegetation meet. California adopted stronger fire-resistant building codes for these zones in 2008. Homes built after 2008 in WUI areas are required to use ember-resistant vents, fire-rated materials, and other protective features. If your home was built before 2008, it may not meet current fire safety standards.
How to Find Out Your Wall & Window Type
1. Touch and look at the exterior walls
Run your hand along an exterior wall. Rough, sandy, and hard = stucco. Smooth plastic panels = vinyl siding. Rough wooden planks with visible grain and paint = wood siding. Brick pattern = masonry. Flat panels with a slight texture, painted = possibly fiber cement.
2. Look at the corners and edges
The edges of the house often reveal the material. Wood siding has visible end grain at the corners. Stucco wraps continuously. Fiber cement panels have clean, flat cut edges. Vinyl siding has a slight hollow sound if you knock on it.
3. Check the LA County Assessor website
Go to assessor.lacounty.gov and search your address. The property record often lists exterior wall construction type and year built — both useful for this form.
4. For windows — look at the frame edge
Open a window and look at the edge of the glass from the side. Single-pane glass is one thin layer. Dual-pane has two layers of glass with a visible gap (often a spacer bar) between them. Dual-pane windows are noticeably thicker. If your home was built before 1990 and windows haven't been replaced, they are likely single-pane.
5. Ask your building manager or check permits
If you rent, ask your property manager. If you own, you can look up building permits at ladbs.org (LA Department of Building and Safety) to see what materials were used in original construction or renovations.
